## Course description

This course covers central topics in epistemology — the theory of knowledge.
It begins with traditional topics like skepticism and certainty, perception and
memory, and the problem of induction, but also includes newer topics like
social epistemology and probability. I hope to challenge your beliefs and
encourage us all to think deeper about what it takes for an individual to know
something, for a community to know something, and why we should care about
knowledge as opposed to mere opinion.

The course aims to introduce students to a broad range of topics in
epistemology, and to develop skills in expressing thoughts clearly in both
spoken and written form — including expressing arguments for a view, and
analysing arguments for and against it.

There is no assigned book. Readings were posted or linked on the class website,
or sent by email.

## Grading and assessment

A quarter of the overall grade each from weekly reading quizzes, presentations,
writing assignments during the semester, and the final paper.

#### Quizzes

Each week had an assigned reading — difficult, but hopefully not too long, so
that you could think deeply about it and look up any unfamiliar words, names,
or ideas mentioned in it. Reading comprehension quizzes on Tuesdays sometimes
asked about these tangential ideas, to make sure you had been looking them up.
I want you to develop habits of intellectual curiosity: to learn to read by
looking beyond just the text itself.

*After the move online*, weekly quizzes were replaced by participation in
discussion forums. By 9:30 pm Sunday you posted a new thread on the week's
reading; by 9:30 am Tuesday you commented on someone else's. No threads were
visible until you had created one of your own. Literally anything you wrote
earned credit — but other students would read it and want to respond.

#### Presentations

Every Thursday we had student presentations. After some class discussion of the
topics from Tuesday's reading, one member of each group was chosen at random to
present on their topic: about five minutes, followed by about five minutes of
discussion. Groups were then randomly reassigned and given topics for the
following week.

Groups were expected to communicate over the weekend to find a time to meet and
outline the presentation. All members took part, because you didn't know which
one of you would be presenting.

#### Writing assignments

Writing a philosophy paper is a difficult task, quite different from most other
writing you have done. So rather than starting with a full paper, the
assignments progress through levels, like in a video game. For each assignment
you write a paper at the level you're on — the idea being that you are always
working on a task challenging enough to be interesting, but not so challenging
as to be frustrating. Each paper was marked "complete", "almost", "good
effort", or "not much progress". Once an assignment was marked complete, you
moved up a level. The final grade depended on how far you had progressed by the
fifth assignment.

| | Complete | Almost | Good effort | Not much progress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Level 3** | A+ | A | A− | B+ |
| **Level 2** | B | B− | C+ | C |
| **Level 1** | C− | D | D− | F |

Each assignment covered a new topic, preferably one discussed since the last
one — you were not rewriting previous assignments.

**Level 1** — short expository paper, 150–300 words. Choose one particular
argument from the readings and explain it. Remember that in philosophy an
"argument" means not just a claim or a view or a theory, but the *reason* for
believing it. It helps to write out the premises and conclusion explicitly in
your notes, so you know exactly what you need to support or criticise.

**Level 2** — expository paper, 400–600 words. Explain *two* related arguments.
They might both be from the readings, or one from the readings and one from
class discussion. They should have an important connection: one might refute a
premise of the other, or extend it, or stand in some other interesting
relationship. Because this paper has some complexity, write an introductory
paragraph once the body is done, explaining what the paper will do and how the
arguments relate.

**Level 3** — expository and critical paper, 700–1000 words. At this point
you're ready to do original work: explain two related arguments as in a Level 2
paper, then give a *new* argument of your own showing that one of them is
unsound — that it either has a false premise, or the premises fail to support
the conclusion. Consider how the original author might respond, and defend your
argument.

The final paper was a Level 3 paper on any topic from the semester, graded on
an ordinary letter scale.

## Schedule of topics

#### Skepticism (January 14, 16)

- Muhammad Al Ghazālī, [*Deliverance from Error*](http://www.crvp.org/publications/Series-IIA/IIA-2-Contents.pdf), introduction and chapter I (1115) — [another translation](https://ghazali.org/books/md/gz101.htm), in which the introduction is labelled "Quoth the Imam Ghazali" and chapter I "The Subterfuges of the Sophists"
- René Descartes, [*Meditations on First Philosophy*](http://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/descartes1641_1.pdf), Meditation 1, pages 1–3 (1641)
- Further reading: [Zhuangzi](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Zhuangzi)
- Video: Wi-Phi, [Jennifer Nagel on skepticism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PqjdRAERWLc)

#### The trilemma of foundations (January 21, 23)

- Hans Albert, *Treatise on Critical Reason*, chapter 1 (1968). Read sections 1 and 2 (pages 12–21) thoroughly, and section 4 (pages 28–38). We won't focus on section 3, but you may find it interesting. Nothing from the footnotes is quizzed, but they are helpful starting points for learning about important developments in twentieth-century philosophy.
- Further reading: Roderick Chisholm, ["The Myth of the Given"](http://joelvelasco.net/teaching/4330/chisolm-mythofgiven.pdf) (1964)
- Video: Wi-Phi, [Kevin McCain on the epistemic regress](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MAZ8awuILJg), discussing foundationalism, infinitism, and coherentism

#### Sense perception (January 28, 30)

- Susanna Siegel, "Cognitive Penetrability and Perceptual Justification" (2012)
- In-class demonstrations: [synesthesia](https://www.theverge.com/2017/12/4/16733778/noisy-gif-hearing-movement-auditory-neuroscience-synesthesia) · [recognition](https://www.newscientist.com/article/mg19325971-600-hybrid-images-now-you-see-them/) · [attention](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vJG698U2Mvo) · [change](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1nL5ulsWMYc) · [colour](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5030741/figure/fig1-2041669516663751/) · [theoretical perception](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VFVZU2YwwJ4) · ["things you can't unsee"](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2014/05/10-things-you-cant-unsee-and-what-that-says-about-your-brain/361335/)

- Further reading: Lewis Carroll, ["What the Tortoise Said to Achilles"](https://www.uni-siegen.de/fb6/phima/lehre/phima10/quellentexte/carroll-tortoise.pdf) (1895)

#### The analysis of knowledge (February 11, 13)

- Videos: Wi-Phi on [the Gettier problem](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5lB-XJjmvoE), [no false lemmas and no defeaters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VAt9h6PCnEg), [causal and reliabilist theories](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3z8sDiaY65Y), and [Geoff Pynn on tracking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lyE0xiMjaoI)
